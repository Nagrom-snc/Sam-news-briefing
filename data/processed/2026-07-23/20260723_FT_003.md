How we made it: China’s air pollution clean-up — and the rebound
Steven Bernard 6-7 minutes 21/07/2026

As the world’s largest source of greenhouse gases annually, China’s progress is the key to curbing the global warming behind the extreme weather being experienced across the world.

Back in 2018, a deep dive into air quality of the then most populous country after its ‘airpocalypse’ in the winter of 2013 showed that while some progress had been made, there was a long way to go.

Fast forward eight years and it was time to see how much it had changed.

The vast nation dramatically reduced its harmful fine particulate matter (PM2.5), with levels falling almost 60 per cent from their 2013 peak by the end of last year.

This led to a 30 per cent fall in the share of hospital admissions caused by this pollution, which comes mainly from vehicle exhaust and industrial emissions.

The particulate matter PM2.5, so-called because it measures 2.5 microns across, is 30 times less than a human hair. It can penetrate deep into the lungs, working into the bloodstream to damage organs around the body.

Much of the improvement in air quality followed Beijing pollution directives, which spurred China’s drive to be a world leader in renewable power sources as it met its ever-growing energy needs.

But its continued reliance on coal, the dirtiest fuel, for more than half of its power generation, makes it an ongoing challenge.

In the first five months of this year, the PM2.5 levels rose an average of 11 per cent across six northern Chinese cities, according to data from the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (Crea), while improvements elsewhere in the country slowed. This coincided with the Middle East war, encouraging the sourcing of cheaper, if more polluting, energy.

The uptick has been linked to a combination of stagnant weather, rising coal production, and strong demand from China’s energy-intensive chemicals industry, which has received policy support as Beijing seeks to localise supply chains, all affecting its citizens air quality.
How we made it

To visualise how much the atmosphere had improved across China over the past decade, I approached the Crea. It is an independent think-tank researching energy and air pollution, based in Helsinki.

Their researchers provide data on a range of emissions, air quality and energy with easy to use trackers: you can find them here. They were able to provide annual data for the 31 provincial capitals in China from 2015.

I decided the best way to show this data was to use a technique called “small multiples”. Arranging the charts into a grid of “mini charts” allowed us to see trends in multiple provinces at the same time.

This is a great technique to try when you want to understand spatial and temporal trends in a single visualisation.

You can see quite clearly the industrial north-east — which had some of the highest levels of pollution — having the greatest success in lowering PM2.5 over the past decade. Meanwhile, the south of the country also made improvements, albeit from a lower starting point.

Another way to visualise the improvement in air quality was to map the change in PM2.5 pollution from 2018 to 2024. The data comes from the ChinaHighPM2.5 database, which has annual observations from 2000.

Using a blue to red diverging colour palette makes it easy to identify which areas have seen the greatest improvement — the north China plain and Xinjiang region in the north-west — and which areas had worsened, including Harbin in Heilongjiang province in the far north-east

Pairing this map with another map showing the actual PM2.5 levels in 2024, and overlaying the areas that were still above the country’s national targets, highlighted that great improvements have been made — but many of the most populated cities were still above the threshold set by Beijing.

For the final graphic, I wanted to show how many people are affected by the different levels of pollution across the country.

To do this, I used both the ChinaHighPM2.5 data and population data from WorldPop. Both data sets are provided as raster (or pixel grid) files at 1km resolution which is important for making the calculations outlined below.

[Mini nerd note: 1km resolution means that one pixel in the data file represents 1km distance on the ground]

[Big nerd note: To create the pollution bands, I used Qgis’ built in raster calculator to output files based on 5μg/m³ ranges for each band. The resulting files are simple black and white rasters where black means the condition has been met, white where it hasn’t. These rasters are then converted into vector polygons and used as a mask layer to query a population count layer for China. This is done using the ‘zonal statistics’ function in Qgis which provides you with a total count for the area.]

Using two different approaches with the same data helps to give a more complete picture — using a map to show where people are affected and the chart to show how many.

Plotting these values for both 2015 and 2025 helps to show that there has been a huge shift in the distribution of population affected by higher pollution levels (right-hand side of the chart) to the lower end of the scale (left-hand side of the chart).

My data colleague in Hong Kong, Haohsiang Ko, also analysed data from the first five months of this year, combined with reporting from Beijing-based colleague Eleanor Olcott.

This showed the hardest challenge for China will remain its reliance on coal. Its abundant cheap supply means it still generates 50 per cent of its energy by burning the most harmful fossil fuel, both to people and plant.

“China’s environmental authoritarianism has the advantage of being able to improve air quality dramatically,” Yanzhong Huang, a senior fellow for global health at the Council on Foreign Relations. “But rising air pollution in some Chinese cities highlights the fragility of gains in pollution control.”
