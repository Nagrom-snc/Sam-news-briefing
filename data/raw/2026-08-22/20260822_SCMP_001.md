Pay, perks, equity: China’s AI, chip firms lead way in offering rewards to lock in talent
Ann Cao Published: 3:00pm, 22 Aug 2026
 www.scmp.com/tech/big-tech/article/3364689/pay-perks-equity-chinas-ai-chip-firms-lead-way-offering-rewards-lock-talent

Companies are rolling out an unprecedented wave of stock grants amid fierce domestic headhunting and an intensifying US-China tech race

In the high-stakes battle for China’s top talent, domestic tech companies – especially in semiconductors and artificial intelligence – are rolling out aggressive equity incentive plans as they capitalise on a bull market to retain key personnel.

Recent corporate filings revealed an unprecedented wave of stock grants, ranging from zero-cost share awards to near-blanket workforce coverage, all aimed at locking in critical talent amid fierce domestic headhunting and the intensifying US-China tech race.

Which company is the most generous? The answer depends on whether generosity is measured by total shares granted, individual fortune-making, or how far down the rewards extend to employees. Here’s a breakdown across sectors.

AI chipmaker Cambricon Technologies is the latest to deliver massive windfalls to employees. Earlier this week, it unlocked nearly 600,000 shares for 124 core staff – yielding an average stock value of 5.57 million yuan (US$828,000) per person, based on its share price on announcement day.

It also rolled out a 5 million share grant last month for 944 employees under its recent incentive plan through 2028, covering 85.3 per cent of its total workforce.

Zhongji InnoLight, a leading producer of optical transceivers used in AI data centres and a regular issuer of equity schemes since 2017, offers a higher yield.

In a recent vesting cycle, 99 key personnel – including senior executives, mid-level managers and core technical staff – were allocated 2.48 million shares. Based on its stock price on the announcement day in April, average yield exceeded 26 million yuan per person.

If inclusivity is the measure, semiconductor equipment maker Advanced Micro-Fabrication Equipment China, known as AMEC, may be the most generous. Its latest restricted stock plan, unveiled in March, covers more than 97 per cent of its total workforce.

Meanwhile, Beijing-based graphics processing unit developer Moore Threads in April brought 1,080 key members into its incentive pool, roughly 85 per cent of its workforce.

Some executives are even digging into their own pockets. Zhu Yiming, founder of memory giant ChangXin Memory Technologies, known as CXMT, has pledged 50 per cent of his partnership stake – 768 million shares – for employee incentives over a decade, explicitly excluding himself.

Pure-play AI companies are using pre-initial public offering (IPO) equity and zero-barrier grants, with MiniMax and Zhipu AI, which listed in Hong Kong earlier this year, appearing the most aggressive.

Beijing-based Zhipu, also known as Z.ai, has set up two shareholding platforms in 2021, one for core staff and another mainly for third-party advisers. The former covers 426 employees, holding a 9.8 per cent post-listing stake – translating to an average holding value of over HK$100 million based on Zhipu’s latest share price. The latter involves 25 participants, with average holdings approaching HK$1.3 billion apiece.

Unlike most companies that tie incentives to performance, Shanghai-based MiniMax offered employees a low entry barrier. In June, it announced its first stock incentive plan since listing, granting 1.16 million shares at zero cost to key personnel and service providers. These awards vest solely on the duration of service, from a few months to years, completely free of performance targets.

Established tech conglomerates – from Alibaba Group Holding and Tencent Holdings to Meituan, Xiaomi and Baidu – are also overhauling incentive playbooks to prevent a talent drain to start-ups.

Last week, Tencent announced it would grant over 38.6 million shares to employee participants, representing 0.42 per cent of its total issued stock, as part of its award scheme set out in 2023.

Alibaba is prioritising financial stability. In May, it said it increased the proportion of long-term cash incentives after “considering the overall trends in the macroeconomic environment and the talent market”, reflecting a strategy to shield top talent from stock price volatility and offering immediate liquidity.

During the March quarter, its share-based compensation expenses dropped 10 per cent year on year to 3.1 billion yuan. Alibaba owns the South China Morning Post.
