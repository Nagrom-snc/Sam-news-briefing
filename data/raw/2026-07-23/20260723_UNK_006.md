Xi's AI power play

Also, critical minerals, AliExpress, and the Politburo

Welcome to Red Thread, Euractiv's weekly newsletter on the EU's relationship with China and the wider Asia-Pacific. I'm Christina Zhao in Oceania, joined by Anupriya Datta in Europe. This week, we decode Chinese President Xi Jinping's keynote AI speech...

Thread of the week

President Xi Jinping opened his keynote at China's flagship AI conference by crediting the US with inventing artificial intelligence. He then spent the rest of the speech arguing that China should shape its future, describing AI as "an international public good that benefits humanity."

Key takeaways, and what they mean for Europe:

China wants to be the "open" AI power

Xi reiterated Beijing's commitment to "open-source development, collaboration and sharing," reinforcing China's attempt to contrast its approach with US export controls on advanced chips and AI models. China wants to be seen as the more accessible AI partner, offering increasingly capable models at lower cost – much as its carmakers have done in the electric vehicle market. For Europe, which lacks a homegrown frontier AI model, Xi's speech may ease concerns that China is preparing to restrict overseas access to its most advanced systems, just weeks after Reuters reported Beijing was considering such measures.

The Global South is the real audience

Xi warned against creating "new historical injustices" through unequal access to AI, framing the technology divide as a moral rather than commercial issue. His message – that AI should not become another technology monopoly controlled by wealthy countries – was aimed as much at Africa, Latin America and Southeast Asia as at Washington.

This is also a challenge to the EU's Global Gateway strategy. Rather than competing only through infrastructure or finance, Beijing is positioning itself as the country willing to share the technologies underpinning the next industrial revolution.

Beijing wants to define the AI age

Xi called for a "widely accepted global AI governance framework" and stronger international coordination, signalling that Beijing wants to shape the rules governing AI. Before unveiling any initiatives, Xi posed four philosophical questions, starting with: "How to get along with thinking machines?"

China's ambition is no longer simply to build leading AI models. Through the World AI Cooperation Organisation (WAICO), new partnerships with the Global South and a growing emphasis on ethics and governance, Beijing is positioning itself as a country that shapes how the AI era is understood and governed.

Brussels has assumed the AI Act would make it the world's leading voice on governance. Xi's speech suggests China now intends to compete for that role by offering its own institutions, language and vision for the AI age.

Security shouldn't become protectionism

Without naming names, Xi argued countries should not use national security to justify restricting technology or disrupting global supply chains.

Xi is seeking to erase the distinction Brussels draws between de-risking and protectionism, particularly in the eyes of developing countries. Yet Beijing has itself shown a willingness to weaponise technology supply chains through export controls on critical minerals and chip-related technologies.

From Asia

All eyes on the Politburo

China's leadership is expected to meet in the coming days for its annual mid-year Politburo gathering, where it will set the economic agenda for the rest of 2026.

European capitals will watch for signs of whether Beijing intends to rebalance towards domestic consumption or continue relying on manufacturing and exports to drive growth. The latter would risk deepening the industrial overcapacity at the heart of the EU's trade disputes with China.

Every word counts: Investors will scour the communiqué for subtle wording changes. In China, adding or dropping a single adjective can move markets.

China keeps squeezing Japan

China's exports of several export-controlled rare earths to Japan remained near zero in June, extending the supply disruption that followed a diplomatic rift over Taiwan last November.

Chinese customs data showed no shipments of gallium, dysprosium, terbium or yttrium last month, underscoring Beijing's willingness to use critical minerals as geopolitical leverage. The restrictions are also helping Chinese manufacturers move up the industrial value chain, with domestic producers gaining market share in high-value materials as Japanese rivals grapple with tighter supplies and higher costs.

For Europe, Japan offers a preview of how Chinese export controls can reshape both supply chains and industrial competitiveness.

The periodic table: Dysprosium takes its name from the Greek dysprositos, meaning "hard to get," a remarkably apt description today.

Kallas in Manila

EU's foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas is in Manila, capital of the Philippines, until Friday to co-chair the EU-ASEAN Post-Ministerial Conference. The visit follows her trip to Brunei in April, as Brussels seeks deeper ties with south-east Asia amid global trade uncertainty.

Kallas said the EU is upgrading strategic partnerships with Vietnam, the Philippines and Indonesia this year. "Three bilateral upgrades within months of each other are not just a coincidence; they are a signal of intent," she said, adding that Singapore will host an EU-ASEAN summit next year.

Ten and rising: Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand and Vietnam together make up ASEAN, the world's fifth-largest economy.

EU lawmakers return to Beijing

An eight-member delegation from the European Parliament's foreign affairs committee began a three-day visit to Beijing and Shanghai on Tuesday, marking the third group of MEPs to travel to China this year.

Engin Eroglu, chair of Parliament's China delegation, described talks with Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi as "a clear sign" Beijing wants to improve relations with the EU. He said discussions were candid despite persistent disagreements, including over trade.

The delegation also met representatives of China's National People's Congress, the Communist Party's International Department, and Shanghai tech companies and local officials to discuss AI, technological sovereignty and competition.

World's biggest legislature: The National People's Congress has almost 3,000 delegates, more than four times the size of the European Parliament.

From Europe

Merz joins Macron on China

France and Germany have called for new "emergency measures" to shield European industry from surging Chinese imports, marking a further convergence in their approach to Beijing.

Following a joint cabinet meeting in Cologne on Friday, President Emmanuel Macron said Paris and Berlin had "never been as aligned on the question of China," while Chancellor Friedrich Merz warned the EU's trade deficit with China "cannot carry on like this." The pair said their finance ministers would present safeguard proposals in September, while insisting they were not seeking to decouple from China.

AliExpress hit with €550m fine

The European Commission has fined Chinese ecommerce platform AliExpress €550 million for failing to do enough to tackle illegal, unsafe and counterfeit products under the Digital Services Act, Anupriya Datta reported. The penalty, the largest imposed under the DSA, follows an investigation launched in 2024. AliExpress called the fine "disproportionate" and said it would appeal.

JD.com hears EU concerns

Another Chinese ecommerce giant facing EU scrutiny is JD.com, which received a formal statement of objections from the Commission on Wednesday over its proposed acquisition of a German retailer. The notice sets out the Commission's concerns under its foreign subsidies investigation, launched in May, and gives JD.com an opportunity to respond and offer commitments.

Hungary probes BYD investment

Hungary has opened an investigation into Chinese electric vehicle maker BYD's investment in the country after former Foreign Minister Péter Szijjártó quit parliament last week to join the company in an executive role, prompting accusations of a conflict of interest.

Prime Minister Péter Magyar told lawmakers on Monday that the probe would examine state support, tax breaks, permits and other commitments linked to the project. He argued that Szijjártó – a close ally of former Prime Minister Viktor Orbán – had backed BYD with extensive public funding and government support while in office.

Also on Euractiv

Opinion: Europe's search for profitable investment should lead to India

China no longer offers the investment opportunities that have long made it the default destination for European companies. Overcapacity and price wars have eroded margins and now European boards are looking elsewhere to build profits. With its vast consumer market and underdeveloped manufacturing base, India is the obvious answer, writes Euractiv columnist Alicia García Herrero. But India will miss the opportunity unless it offers stronger investor protections and opens public procurement.

Quebec courts Europe in bid to de-risk from US, China

Quebec wants to deepen economic ties with Europe as US tariffs and China's critical minerals export controls expose the risks of relying on concentrated supply chains, the province's international affairs minister Christopher Skeete told Thomas Moller-Nielsen. He argued the resource-rich province could help reduce Europe's dependence on Chinese critical minerals.
